Repair or Replace?

When Is It Worth Considering a New AC System?

A broken AC doesn't automatically mean you need a new system. Replacement becomes a useful conversation when the current repair is only one part of a larger equipment, comfort or cost problem.

Factors worth weighing together

  • The nature and cost of the current repair
  • Repeated failures or rising repair spending
  • Overall equipment condition
  • Comfort or humidity problems that continue even when the system is running
  • Efficiency and operating-cost concerns
  • Parts or refrigerant considerations that affect the practical repair path
  • Changes to the home or your comfort needs
  • What you expect from the next system

Age is useful context, but it shouldn't make the decision by itself. ENERGY STAR suggests considering replacement when an air conditioner or heat pump is more than 10 years old, particularly when other performance or repair issues are present; the actual decision still depends on the system and the home.

System Design

The New System Should Be Selected for Your Home

Replacement is a chance to correct problems rather than automatically reproduce the old system. Equipment capacity should be based on the home's actual heating and cooling requirements, not simply the tonnage of the unit being removed or a square-footage rule of thumb.

A replacement evaluation should consider what affects load and comfort, then determine the right capacity and configuration. ENERGY STAR recommends sizing from the home's actual characteristics and identifies Manual J as the calculation used to determine heating and cooling load.

Confirm Catalyst's actual design process (Manual J, Manual S, duct or static-pressure testing). State it precisely, or don't claim it.

What should be evaluated

  • Home size, layout and relevant construction characteristics
  • Existing equipment and how it has performed
  • Cooling load and proper equipment sizing
  • Duct system and available airflow
  • Hot or cold rooms and persistent comfort issues
  • Humidity concerns
  • Thermostat and control needs
  • Electrical or other conditions that affect the project
  • Your priorities for comfort, efficiency, noise and budget

Choosing a System

Compare More Than a Single Efficiency Number

Efficiency matters, but it's only one part of the decision. You may also want to compare system type, capacity control, comfort characteristics, controls, equipment matching, warranty and installed cost.

Option

Traditional Single-Stage Systems

A conventional system can be the right fit when the priority is straightforward cooling at a lower equipment-cost tier. It still needs to be properly sized and matched to the home.

Option

Multi-Stage and Variable-Capacity Systems

Systems that run at more than one capacity level can offer a different comfort and operating profile than equipment that only runs at full capacity. Actual benefits depend on the equipment, system design, controls and installation.

Option

Heat Pump Options

A heat pump provides both cooling and heating and may be worth comparing with a conventional AC and furnace setup, depending on the home's existing equipment, energy sources and your priorities.

The Installation Matters

New Equipment Can Only Perform as Well as the System Around It

The equipment is only part of the project. ENERGY STAR's quality-installation guidance highlights proper sizing, system airflow, refrigerant charge and duct evaluation, which connect the equipment choice to how the complete system will operate.

Is the equipment properly sized?

Oversized equipment can cycle too often and create comfort, humidity and equipment-life problems. Bigger isn't automatically better.

Can the duct system deliver the required airflow?

A new condenser and air handler don't fix every duct or airflow problem. Existing duct conditions should be considered where they affect performance.

Are the indoor and outdoor components properly matched and commissioned?

Components, refrigerant charge, airflow and controls need to work together as one system.

Beyond the Old System

Use Replacement to Address the Problems You Actually Want Solved

A new system shouldn't be chosen only because the old one failed. The replacement conversation can also identify persistent comfort problems you want the new system to address:

  • Rooms that are consistently hard to cool
  • Excessive cycling
  • Humidity concerns
  • Uneven airflow
  • Equipment noise
  • Thermostat and control preferences
  • Energy-efficiency goals
  • Future plans for the home

Some comfort problems start outside the equipment. ENERGY STAR notes that ducts, insulation and air leakage can contribute to hot and cold rooms and system performance. Where those conditions matter, the replacement plan should identify them rather than imply that higher-efficiency equipment automatically fixes the house.

AC Replacement & Installation Questions

How do I know whether to repair or replace my AC?
Consider the current repair, recurring failures, equipment condition, comfort, efficiency, parts availability and the economics of the options. Age is useful context, but it shouldn't be the only reason to replace a system. If you don't yet know whether replacement is necessary, start with an AC service evaluation.
Should I replace my AC with the same size system I have now?
Not automatically. The existing size is useful information, but replacement sizing should reflect the home's actual heating and cooling requirements. Changes to the home, earlier sizing decisions, duct conditions and comfort history can all matter.
What is SEER2?
SEER2 is the current seasonal efficiency rating for central air-conditioning equipment under updated federal test procedures. It's useful for comparing rated cooling efficiency, but on its own it doesn't predict your utility bill or guarantee comfort.
Is a bigger AC system better in Houston?
No. Oversizing can cause frequent cycling and contribute to comfort and humidity problems. Capacity should come from the home's needs, not from choosing a larger unit because Houston summers are hot.
Should I consider a heat pump?
A heat pump provides both cooling and heating and can be one option to compare when replacing a central AC. Whether it makes sense depends on the home's current heating equipment, energy sources, system design, budget and your priorities.
Do I need to replace my ductwork when I replace the AC?
Not necessarily. Existing ducts should be evaluated where their condition, sizing, leakage or airflow may affect the new system. Some homes need duct modifications or repairs; others don't. The recommendation should follow the actual condition of the system.
